# Campaign concept

A campaign starts with a territory, not an asset. This produces the territory, the options
around it, and the board that lets people see it. Everything built afterwards inherits it.

---

## Before anything: this is not an automation step

A concept assembled from what you have already published is, by construction, an average of
past work. That is the opposite of what a campaign needs.

**The marketer's direction is the input.** Get these first:

1. **What the campaign is actually about** — the argument, not the topic
2. **The angle** — not "AI in banking" but what *we* say about it that others don't
3. **The emotion** — what the reader feels today, in their job. **Ask; never offer a menu.**
   Offering "is it frustration?" gets agreement, not information.
4. **What must not happen** — the version of this you would be embarrassed by

If these are thin, **stop and work them out in conversation.** A board built on a thin brief
looks finished and is worthless. Say so plainly rather than proceeding politely.

---

## Step 1 — What territory do you already own?

Ask the `find-assets` skill (or the requester) for recent campaigns. Bring back:

- **Territories already used**, and how recently. Don't re-run a look the audience saw six
  months ago.
- **What is still live.** Anything concurrent sits inside it or consciously differs from it.
- **What performed**, where results exist.

If a proposed direction sits close to something recent, name it.

---

## Step 2 — Four territories, not one

Four, deliberately different: **one safe, one obvious, one uncomfortable, one that might be
wrong.** A shortlist of four variations on one idea is not a shortlist.

```
THE IDEA          one line, the way you'd say it out loud
WHY IT WORKS      the argument, tied to the angle and the audience
THE FEELING       what the reader feels seeing it
WHERE IT GOES     how it behaves on a page, an ad, social, an event
WHERE IT BREAKS   the format or moment it would struggle in — be honest
```

**Recommend one, and say why.** A recommendation someone can disagree with is more useful
than four equal options.

---

## Step 3 — Naming shortlist

For the chosen territory, four routes, each with a slogan:

| Route | Character |
|---|---|
| **Clearest** | Says the thing plainly |
| **Forward** | Movement, direction, momentum |
| **Senior** | Built for a boardroom |
| **The full arc** | The whole story in one line |

Mark the recommended one and note which audience each suits — a name that works for a CIO
may not work on a social tile. Check every candidate against the banned words in
`house-rules`.

---

## Step 4 — Name the visual motifs

This is what makes a board usable rather than decorative. Give each visual direction a
**name people can say in a meeting** — *"the light trail"*, *"the split screen"*.

```
NAME              what the team will call it
WHAT IT IS        one sentence
WHAT IT MEANS     why it belongs to this concept, not just that it looks good
WHERE IT'S USED   hero · section break · ad · social · event backdrop
WHAT IT'S NOT     the near-miss to avoid
```

Four to six. Fewer and the campaign has no range; more and nothing is recognisable.

---

## Step 5 — Image prompts

If the company already has an image style, read it from `house-rules` or ask for two or
three reference images and describe the pattern: subject, light, setting, framing.

Write **two or three prompts per motif** in a consistent shape —
**[concrete subject] + [specific quality of light or colour] + [setting] + [framing]**. No
brand names, no logos, no text in the image.

Then say what to check on the way back:

- Does it read at small size, or only as a hero?
- Does it show the motif, or is it just generically moody?
- Are there faces? They need the same scrutiny as stock photography.

**Never generate the images yourself.** Write the prompts; the image account and the taste
belong to the marketer and the design team.

---

## Step 6 — Palette, type and register

- **Palette** — from the company's existing brand colours. Say which accent leads and keep
  accents to a minority of any composition. A new colour is a brand decision, not a concept
  decision — flag it.
- **Type** — the brand typeface; name the treatments this concept implies (oversized,
  outline, masked).
- **How we write, for this campaign** — two or three lines on top of the house voice.
- **Do & don't** — six to eight concrete pairs. Not "be on brand" but *"light emerges from
  dark, never floods it."*

---

## Step 7 — The board

Section order:

```
1  Campaign name + the concept in one line
2  The concept, explained
3  The motifs, with reference imagery
4  Copy on the creative — rough placements to show tone
5  The naming shortlist, recommendation marked
6  Palette
7  How we write
8  Do & don't
9  Timeline
```

Rough placements should **look** rough, and say so on the board. They show tone; they are not
artwork to approve.

---

## Rules

- **Four territories, genuinely different.** Always recommend one.
- **Say where each one breaks.** A concept with no stated weakness hasn't been thought about.
- **Never invent a palette.** Use the brand's, or flag a brand decision.
- **Never generate images.** Write prompts.
- **Name the motifs.**
- **Thin direction → stop.** Don't fill the gap from past content.
